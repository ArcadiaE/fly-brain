FastBrain · 果蝇全脑实时仿真

138,639 个神经元约 1500 万个突触FlyWire v783 连接组消费级 GPU开源

这个仓库把一整只果蝇的脑子搬进了电脑：138,639 个神经元、约 1500 万个突触，全部来自真实连接组测绘数据。模型在一张 6 GB 的游戏显卡上实时运行，你可以给神经元施加刺激，看脉冲怎么传遍全脑，就像给大脑做实验，只不过这只果蝇活在显卡里。

神经科学家在 2024 年发表了第一个这样的全脑模型（Shiu et al., Nature），但原版跑 0.5 秒的脑活动要 26 秒，做实验得等。这个项目把同样的模型加速了 27 倍——0.5 秒的脑活动 0.98 秒算完——并且用一整套验证证明了加速没有损失精度。在此之上还做了两件工具：一个能在浏览器里边跑边操作的实时控制台，和本页下面就能直接玩的交互回放。

在线试玩：三个真实实验的回放

下面是三次真实仿真的完整录像，不是动画演示。每个回放里每个点是一个真实神经元的位置，金色闪光是它放电的瞬间。点播放，拖进度条，旋转大脑，把鼠标悬停在放电的神经元上能看到它的名字和放电频率。底部是几个关键神经元的膜电位波形，和示波器上看到的真实神经活动一个道理。

回放太大打不开？直接在新标签页打开：糖刺激 · P9 前行 · 沉默蘑菇体（每个约 3 MB，数据全部内嵌）
这三个实验在干什么？

糖刺激复现了原论文的经典实验：果蝇腿上的糖味觉感受神经元被激活后，信号经过食道下区传到中枢脑，一路激活几百个神经元。1 秒脑时间里共有 403 个神经元放过电、17,393 次脉冲。

P9 前行刺激的是两个下行通路神经元 P9，原论文证明这能驱动果蝇向前行走。这个实验放电量小得多（130 个神经元、806 次脉冲），因为信号直奔运动输出而去，通路很短。

沉默蘑菇体是干预实验：蘑菇体是果蝇的学习记忆中枢，它的 96 个输出神经元（MBON）被永久沉默（紫色点）后再给糖刺激，全脑脉冲从 17,393 次降到 16,790 次。对比前两个回放，能直观看到一类神经元被拿掉之后信息流向怎么变。这类"在硅基脑里做损毁实验"的能力，正是这个平台的用途。

它是怎么做到的

模型本身不复杂：每个神经元是一个渗漏积分发放单元，膜电位攒到阈值就放一次电，放电沿突触传给下游，突触的强弱和符号全部来自真实连接组。难的是规模——13.8 万个神经元每 0.1 毫秒算一步，原版实现一步要在 GPU 上启动约 75 次计算内核，时间全花在启动开销上。FastBrain 引擎用四招把这个开销压了下去：

事件驱动：只算放电的神经元

每一瞬间放电的神经元只有几百个，占 0.3%。引擎把突触矩阵预先切成 15.1 万个定长小块，每步只把放电神经元的块拣出来做乘加，不再对全图做稠密乘法。

CUDA Graph：一万次启动合成一次

所有张量形状固定之后，连续的 16 步仿真被录制成一张 CUDA Graph，一次启动跑完，每步的内核启动数从约 75 降到约 33。

延迟环：把突触延迟变成编译期常量

突触传递有 1.8 毫秒延迟。引擎用一个环形缓冲区存中间的信号，环长取延迟步数的整数倍，寻址在编译期就确定，运行时零开销。

快的前提是准

加速引擎和原版逐指标对比：放电率相关系数 0.990，而原版自己换个随机种子重跑的相关也只有 0.991。也就是说两个引擎的差异，和同一模型换颗骰子的差异一样大。验证细节见下面"准不准"一节。

在自己电脑上跑一遍

仓库带一个实时控制台：模型在 GPU 上跑，浏览器里同步看到全脑放电，可以随手加刺激、挂探针、暂停重置。装环境大约十分钟（Python + CUDA 版 PyTorch），双击 run_live.bat 就能打开。

控制台全貌：左边选细胞类型加刺激，中间是全脑放电动画，底部示波器是 8 个神经元的实时膜电位。

FastBrain · Real-time Fly Whole-Brain Simulation

138,639 neurons~15M synapsesFlyWire v783 connectomeconsumer GPUopen source

This repository puts an entire fruit fly brain into a computer: 138,639 neurons and about 15 million synapses, all taken from real connectome mapping data. The model runs in real time on a 6 GB gaming GPU. You can stimulate neurons and watch the pulses spread across the whole brain — like doing an experiment on a brain, except this fly lives inside a graphics card.

Neuroscientists published the first such whole-brain model in 2024 (Shiu et al., Nature), but the original takes 26 seconds to simulate half a second of brain activity, which makes experiments painful to run. This project makes the same model 27 times faster — half a second of brain activity in 0.98 seconds — and proves with a full validation harness that the speedup costs nothing in accuracy. On top of that it adds two tools: a live console you can operate in a browser while the simulation runs, and the interactive replays you can play with right below.

Try it: replays of three real experiments

Below are complete recordings of three real simulations, not animations. Every dot is a real neuron's position, and each gold flash is the moment it fires. Press play, scrub the timeline, rotate the brain, and hover over firing neurons to see their names and rates. The traces at the bottom are membrane voltages of a few key neurons — the same kind of signal an electrophysiologist sees on an oscilloscope.

Trouble loading? Open the replays in a new tab: sugar · P9 walking · mushroom body silenced (about 3 MB each, all data embedded)
What are these three experiments?

Sugar reproduces the paper's classic experiment: activating the sugar gustatory receptor neurons on the fly's leg sends the signal through the subesophageal zone into the central brain, recruiting a few hundred neurons along the way. Over one second of brain time, 403 neurons fire a total of 17,393 spikes.

P9 walking stimulates two descending-pathway neurons called P9, which the original paper showed can drive forward walking. Much less of the brain lights up (130 neurons, 806 spikes) because the signal runs straight to the motor output over a very short pathway.

Silenced mushroom body is an intervention: the mushroom body is the fly's center of learning and memory. With its 96 output neurons (MBONs) permanently silenced (purple dots), the same sugar stimulus produces 16,790 spikes instead of 17,393. Comparing the two replays shows how information flow changes when one class of neurons is removed. Doing lesion experiments inside a silicon brain is exactly what this platform is for.

How it works

The model itself is simple: each neuron is a leaky integrate-and-fire unit that fires when its membrane voltage crosses a threshold, and the spike travels down its synapses, whose strengths and signs all come from the real connectome. The hard part is scale — 138,639 neurons stepping every 0.1 millisecond, with the original implementation launching about 75 GPU kernels per step, spending nearly all its time on launch overhead. The FastBrain engine cuts that overhead four ways:

Event-driven: only compute the neurons that fire

Only a few hundred neurons fire at any instant, about 0.3% of the brain. The synapse matrix is pre-cut into 151,000 fixed-size blocks; each step gathers only the blocks belonging to firing neurons, instead of multiplying against the whole graph.

CUDA Graph: ten thousand launches become one

With every tensor pinned to a fixed shape, 16 consecutive simulation steps are recorded into a single CUDA graph and launched once, cutting kernel launches from about 75 to about 33 per step.

Delay ring: synaptic delay as a compile-time constant

Synaptic transmission takes 1.8 ms. The engine stores in-flight signals in a ring buffer whose length is a multiple of the delay in steps, so addressing is resolved at capture time and costs nothing at run time.

Fast only counts if it stays accurate

Compared metric by metric against the original: firing-rate correlation 0.990, while the original compared against itself with a different random seed reaches 0.991. The two engines differ by exactly as much as the same model re-rolled with a new die. Details in the accuracy section below.

How fast, exactly

Same RTX 3060 Laptop, same experiment (sugar stimulation at 200 Hz, 0.5 s simulated), same timing harness:

BackendRunsSim timeReal-time ratio

A ratio of 0.51× means one second of brain time costs two seconds of wall time. A stronger GPU (an RTX 5080, say) goes past real time. Full timing data lives in data/results/local_fastbrain/.

Is it accurate: checked line by line against the original

The validation covers a full matrix of 2 stimuli × 2 durations × 5 random seeds × 3 time steps, plus a 10-second long-run drift check. The bar for every metric is not "close enough" — the difference must be smaller than the noise the reference model produces against itself just by changing its random seed:

Run it on your own machine

The repository ships a live console: the model runs on your GPU while a browser shows the whole brain spiking in sync, and you can add stimuli, attach voltage probes, pause and reset at will. Setup takes about ten minutes (Python plus a CUDA build of PyTorch); double-click run_live.bat and the console opens.
